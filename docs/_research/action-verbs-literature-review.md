## Part A — Verbatim verb lists per authoritative source

### A1. UN DGACM — official "Editing of resolutions at the United Nations" (delegate guidance, with Annex)
Source: https://www.un.org/en/ga/second/72/editingguidelines.pdf (same text reissued for many sessions, e.g. /second/69/, /third/78/). This is the closest thing to an *official* UN-Secretariat verb list — produced by the DGACM Editing Section that actually edits every draft resolution.

Structural rules (verbatim):
- "Preambular paragraphs ... **must begin with a verb in the form of a present, past or perfect participle or an adjective in italics.**"
- "Operative paragraphs are numbered ... **must begin with an action verb.**"
- "When the opening verb of a paragraph is repeated in the following paragraph(s), the word **also** is used. If the verb is repeated a second time in succession, the word **further** is used. Also and further are placed **after** the verb in the preamble and **before** the verb in the operative part." (Note: this contradicts several MUN guides that say "further" second, "also" third — see disagreements section.)

**Annex — Common preambular verbs and adjectives (verbatim list):**
> Acknowledging · Affirming · Alarmed · Aware · Bearing in mind · Believing · Cognizant · Commending · Concerned, Gravely concerned, Deeply concerned (about) · Conscious · Convinced · Deploring · Disturbed, Deeply disturbed · Emphasizing · Expressing (alarm, appreciation, concern, gratitude, satisfaction) · Guided · Having considered, Having heard, Having received · Mindful · Noting, Noting with concern, Noting with appreciation, Noting with satisfaction · Reaffirming · Realizing · Recalling · Recognizing · Stressing · Taking into account · Taking note, Taking note with appreciation, Taking note with concern · Thanking · Underlining, Underscoring the fact that · Urging

**Annex — Common operative verbs (verbatim list):**
> Accepts · Adopts · Affirms · Agrees · Appeals · Appreciates · Approves · Authorizes · Calls upon · Commends · Condemns, Strongly condemns · Decides · Declares · Demands · Denounces · Deplores · Discourages · Emphasizes · Endorses · Expresses (alarm, appreciation, concern, gratitude, satisfaction) · Invites · Notes, Notes with concern, Notes with appreciation, Notes with satisfaction · Reaffirms · Recognizes · Recommends · Reiterates · Rejects · Renews, Renews its invitation, Renews its commitment · Requests · Supports · Takes note, Takes note with appreciation · Underlines, Underscores the fact that · Urges · Welcomes

Additional DGACM editorial rulings that matter for a taxonomy (verbatim):
- "**Takes note/notes.** In general, 'takes note' is used when the object is a report, statement or decision. 'Notes' is used in the sense of 'observes'."
- "A body should **not take note of or welcome its own past decision.** The editor will ... come up with a more appropriate verb, such as 'recalls' or 'reaffirms'."
- "**Reiterates.** ... it means that the Assembly is **repeating something it has said in a previous resolution.** If the editor can find no such previous text ... 'affirms' or 'stresses' or 'emphasizes'." — i.e. reaffirm/recall/reiterate are formally *anaphoric* (point to prior text); this is an editorial rule, not just style.
- "**Personification.** A resolution ... does not decide/recommend/launch/convene/extend a mandate ... it is the author or body adopting the resolution who takes the action."

### A2. UN DGACM Editorial Manual (online) — res-dec-draft-edit
Source: https://www.un.org/dgacm/en/content/editorial-manual/res-dec-draft-edit
- "Each preambular paragraph begins with a **present, past or perfect participle or participial phrase or an adjective.**" Examples given: Believing, Convinced, Desirous, Expressing confidence, Noting with satisfaction, Taking note, Having examined.
- "Each operative paragraph begins with an **operative verb (or phrase) in the present indicative tense.**" Examples: Decides, Recommends, Expresses its appreciation, Requests, Also requests, Expresses the hope, Takes note with satisfaction, Calls upon, Calls for.
- Formatting rule: only the *opening* operative verb/phrase of a paragraph is italicized.

### A5. Legal Response International — "Legal interpretation of verbs" (COP/UNFCCC context, but general treaty-body verb strength)
Source: https://legalresponse.org/resource/legal-interpretation-of-verbs/
Explicit legal strength ranking (verbatim gist):
- **Strongest:** "**Decides**" (and "agrees") — "the strongest of the verbs commonly used in UN negotiations," "more likely to indicate that the [body's] decision is attempting to create a mandatory obligation."
- Discretionary tier, strongest→softest: **Urges** > **Requests** > **Recommends**. Verbatim: "'urges' would usually be interpreted as the stronger recommendation than 'recommends'"; "'requests' is softer than 'urges' and implies a greater degree of discretion"; "recommends" is "the softest obligation" of the three.
- Also: "**shall**" = mandatory term; "**may**" = permissive; "**should**" = hortatory.

### A6. IPI Security Council Compliance research (Bosco/IPI) & compliance literature
Source: https://www.ipinst.org/wp-content/uploads/2013/12/pdfs_Guidelines_for_Evaluating_Compliance_with_UN_Security_Council_Resolutions_final.pdf (fetch 403'd; content via search summary)
- Codes SC operative clauses as "demands" for compliance measurement; four-point compliance scale (no/marginal → full). Confirms verb hierarchy in coding: "**requested** is less commanding than **demanded**"; "binding paragraphs start with **decides** instead of **urges, invites or calls upon**."

## Part B — Legal bindingness dimension (scholarship)

### B1. ICJ Namibia Advisory Opinion (1971) — the foundational legal authority
Source: https://www.icj-cij.org/index.php/node/103754 ; analysis in Wood (below), paras 108–114.
Key holding (verbatim, quoted in Wood): "**The language of a resolution of the Security Council should be carefully analysed before a conclusion can be made as to its binding effect.** In view of the nature of the powers under Article 25, the question whether they have been in fact exercised is to be determined in each case, having regard to **the terms of the resolution to be interpreted, the discussions leading to it, the Charter provisions invoked and, in general, all circumstances** that might assist in determining the legal consequences of the resolution."
Implication: bindingness is **not** mechanically read off the verb; verb choice is strong evidence but the whole context governs. Also established Article 25 (binding decisions) is **not** confined to Chapter VII.

### B2. Sir Michael C. Wood, "The Interpretation of Security Council Resolutions," Max Planck Yearbook of UN Law 2 (1998) 73–95
Source: https://www.mpil.de/files/pdf2/mpunyb_wood_2.pdf
- On drafting practice (verbatim): a mandatory decision "includes the words '**acting under Chapter VII**' or reference to an appropriate article thereof, as well as the word '**decides**'. But such drafting practices as exist are not always well-known ... nor ... consistently [applied]."
- "Unlike most treaties, **most provisions of SCRs are not intended to create rights and obligations binding on States.** Many are operational or internal to the United Nations or of a political nature."
- On the SG as addressee (verbatim): "Typically the Council **requests the Secretary-General to submit a report,** then considers and approves the report ... and **requests the Secretary-General to implement** that which it has approved." → confirms the actor dimension: "requests + Secretary-General" is the standard *executable* mandate construction (see Part E).
- SCRs are "**often part of a series** and it is only possible to understand them as such" (Namibia read res. 276 with 264 and 269) → supports treating recall/reaffirm/reiterate as cross-resolution links.

### B3. Rosalyn Higgins, "The Advisory Opinion on Namibia: Which UN Resolutions are Binding under Article 25 of the Charter?", ICLQ 21 (1972) 270.
Source: https://academic.oup.com/book/27389/chapter/197186121 (also Cambridge Core). Canonical article naming the interpretive problem; argues bindingness turns on intent evidenced by language + context, per Namibia.

### B4. General consensus in the legal-bindingness literature (multiple sources)
Sources: EJIL:Talk! https://www.ejiltalk.org/legal-bindingness-of-security-council-resolutions-generally-and-resolution-2334-on-the-israeli-settlements-in-particular/ ; EIP explainer https://www.eip.org/eip-explainer-how-to-read-un-security-council-resolutions/ ; Wikipedia UNSC resolution.
- Binding indicator: "**decides**" (esp. with "acting under Chapter VII").
- Non-binding / hortatory indicators: "**calls upon**," "**reaffirms**," "**underlines**," "**stresses**," "**urges**," "**invites**" are "generally understood not to indicate an intention ... to create binding legal obligation."
- Grey area explicitly flagged: "**calls upon**," "**warns**," "**requests**" — no universally agreed force.
- Structural: **General Assembly** resolutions are recommendatory in general (Charter art. 10–14), except internal/budgetary matters (art. 17 — budget decisions ARE binding); **Security Council** alone can bind under art. 25. This is an *organ* dimension overlaying the verb dimension.

### B5. Speech-act / illocutionary-force framing (linguistics scholarship)
Sources: "Speech Acts in UN Treaties: A Pragmatic Perspective" https://www.scirp.org/html/12-1641024_104642.htm ; Searle's taxonomy (assertive/representative, commissive, directive, expressive, declarative) — Stanford Encyclopedia https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/speech-acts/
- Searle's five illocutionary classes map cleanly onto resolution clauses and are a defensible theoretical backbone for the taxonomy's "category" dimension:
  - **Representatives/assertives** → observing verbs (notes, recognizes, considers).
  - **Expressives** → evaluative verbs (welcomes, condemns, deplores, commends).
  - **Directives** → directive verbs (requests, calls upon, urges, demands).
  - **Commissives** → self-binding verbs (decides, resolves, pledges, commits, undertakes).
  - **Declarations** → performative/deciding verbs (adopts, proclaims, declares, establishes) — these *change institutional reality* by being uttered.
- Note: "reinforcing" (reaffirm/recall) is not a Searle class; it is a *repetition/anaphora* operator layered on assertives/expressives (see critique in Part F).

## Part C — Budget & mandate-generation relevance

### C1. Programme Budget Implications (PBI) — Rule 153
Sources: DGACM PBI page https://www.un.org/dgacm/en/content/editorial-manual/pbis ; GA rules of procedure https://www.un.org/en/ga/about/ropga/abq.shtml ; Third Cttee PBI overview https://www.un.org/en/ga/third/78/docs/PBI_Process.pdf
- **Rule 153 (verbatim):** "**No resolution involving expenditure may be recommended by a committee for approval by the General Assembly unless it is accompanied by an estimate of expenditures prepared by the Secretary-General.** No resolution in respect of which expenditures are anticipated shall be voted on by the Assembly until the Fifth Committee has had an opportunity to state the effect of the proposal on the budget."
- ECOSOC analogue: **Rule 31** ("before a proposal involving expenditure is approved by the Council, the Secretary-General must prepare an estimate of the programme budget implications").
- Rule 154 (companion) governs the Fifth Committee/ACABQ machinery. CPC reviews programmatic aspects; ACABQ reviews the money.

### C2. Which constructions trigger budget/reporting consequences (synthesis)
This is the load-bearing part for the mandates project. Recognized *mandate-generating* patterns:
- **"Requests the Secretary-General to report / to submit a report"** → creates a **reporting mandate** (a recurring output obligation, tracked in DGACM's reporting cycle). This is the single most common executable mandate verb+actor pattern (Wood B2; A/60/733 focuses heavily on report mandates).
- **"Requests / decides that the Secretary-General [do X]"**, **"Authorizes"**, **"Establishes [a body/mission/fund]"**, **"Decides to continue/extend [a mandate]"**, **"Approves [the programme budget / a post]"** → resource-generating; almost always carry a PBI.
- **Absorption clauses** — the phrases that *cap or neutralize* budget impact: "**within existing resources**", "**within available resources**", "**from within the approved programme budget**", "**from voluntary/extrabudgetary resources**", "**subject to the availability of resources**." A request accompanied by "within existing resources" still creates a mandate but signals *no new appropriation intended*. **[UNCERTAIN — exact canonical phrase list]**: these phrases are ubiquitous in practice and press releases (e.g. https://press.un.org/en/2024/gaab4483.doc.htm) but I did not find a single official document enumerating them as a closed set; treat as an empirically-derived pattern.
- Merely **rhetorical / non-budgetary** language: preambular verbs; operative "welcomes / condemns / stresses / calls upon [Member States] / urges [States]" — expressive or exhortatory, addressed to actors the Secretariat cannot task, so no PBI.

### C3. Mandate definition — what counts as a mandate
- **A/60/733, "Mandating and delivering" (SG report, 30 Mar 2006):** established the analytical framework for the 2006 mandate review; operationally treats a mandate as a **request/direction for action contained in a resolution or decision of a principal organ** (esp. GA/SC/ECOSOC), with reporting mandates a major subclass. Source: https://www.securitycouncilreport.org/un-documents/document/a60733.php ; https://docs.un.org/en/A/60/733 **[UNCERTAIN — exact one-line definition not captured verbatim this session; recommend pulling the ODS PDF §II for the precise wording.]**
- **UN80 Mandate Implementation Review (2025–26):** current working definition (verbatim from UN80 materials): a mandate is "**a request or directive for action set out in the UN Charter, a resolution or decision by a UN intergovernmental organ.**" Sources: https://www.un.org/un80-initiative/en/frequently-asked-questions ; https://www.un.org/un80-initiative/en/news/report-mandate-implementation-review ; established via GA res. **79/318** (18 Jul 2025) and decision **79/571** (2 Sep 2025), IAHWG co-chaired by Jamaica & New Zealand, outcome ~March 2026. Also CIC/NYU analysis: https://cic.nyu.edu/resources/un80-mandate-review-promising-momentum-to-overcome-the-mandate-maze/
- **Dag Hammarskjöld Library** working definition: a mandate is "the decision that gives a body authority to carry out its functions"; synonyms "terms of reference, founding documents, authorization, approval." Source: https://research.un.org/en/docs/mandates
- Takeaway for the taxonomy: the *deciding* + *directive* categories (addressed to UN organs/Secretariat) are the mandate-generating ones; *observing / reinforcing / evaluative* categories essentially never create a mandate on their own.

## Part D — Force ordering within the directive category (multiple sources agree)

Directive verbs, weakest → strongest (converging across UN.org A3, Legal Response A5, IPI A6, EIP, and MUN guides):

> **invites ≈ suggests ≈ encourages < recommends ≈ requests < calls upon < urges < demands < decides**

Notes and caveats:
- UN.org (A3) and Second-Committee note: requests (neutral) < calls upon < urges < demands (highest, "rarely used").
- Legal Response (A5): within *discretionary* verbs, recommends < requests < urges. "Decides" sits **above** all of these as the mandatory verb (a category jump, not just one more notch).
- **demands** is de facto **Security-Council-only** (highest exhortatory force; several guides say GA/other organs do not use it). **[UNCERTAIN — "only SC" is a strong-convention claim, not a written rule.]**
- **decides** is qualitatively different: it is *commissive/declarative* (the body binds itself or an organ under its authority), not merely directive. Placing it at the top of a single directive scale is convenient but conflates two dimensions (force vs. bindingness) — keep them separate (see Part E/F).
- Security-Council-specific micro-scale (Wood/SC practice): **expresses its intention to … < decides** (an "expresses intention" clause signals future action without present commitment).
- MUN-guide orderings occasionally reorder invites/encourages/recommends among themselves; the *ends* of the scale (invites weakest; demands/decides strongest) are stable.

## Part E — Actor / addressee dimension

No single official document formalizes an addressee typology, but the distinction is strongly and consistently drawn in practice and scholarship:

| Addressee | Typical verb pattern | Nature |
|---|---|---|
| **Secretary-General / Secretariat** | "Requests the Secretary-General to …", "Decides that the SG shall …" | **Executable mandate** — the UN can be directly tasked; generates reporting mandates & PBIs (Wood B2; A/60/733; Rule 153). |
| **Member States (all States / a named State)** | "Calls upon [States] …", "Urges …", "Demands …" (SC) | **Exhortation** (GA) or **binding decision** (SC art. 25). No PBI. Discretion varies with the verb. |
| **Another UN organ / subsidiary body** | "Invites the Security Council to …", "Recommends to the General Assembly …", "Requests the [committee] to …" | **Inter-organ request** — deliberately polite ("invites"/"recommends") because organs cannot command each other; may create a mandate for that organ. |
| **The adopting body itself** | "Decides to …", "Resolves to …", "Pledges / Commits …" | **Self-binding / commissive** — internal decision or political commitment. |

Key sourcing: Wood (B2) on SG-tasking; UN.org strength note and MUN guides on "invites/encourages ... when the GA wants to remain polite, particularly when asking the Security Council to do something"; DGACM personification rule (A1) reinforces that the *body*, not the text, acts. The addressee therefore co-determines both bindingness (SC+States) and budget-relevance (Secretariat).

## Part F — Synthesized master taxonomy proposal

### F1. Dimensions (six)
1. **category** — clause function: `observing` (assertive/representative), `reinforcing` (repetition operator), `evaluative` (expressive), `deciding` (commissive/declarative), `directive`. (Aligns with Searle B5 except `reinforcing`, which is orthogonal.)
2. **force_rank** — ordinal 0–5 within the *directive/deciding* spine (0 = observational/no force; 5 = binding decision). Only meaningful for directive/deciding; evaluative/observing get 0.
3. **sentiment_polarity** — `+` / `0` / `−` (welcomes = +, notes = 0, condemns = −). Mainly discriminates evaluative verbs; most directive/deciding verbs are `0`.
4. **addressee_class** — `secretariat` | `member_states` | `other_organ` | `self` | `n/a` (preamble/general). Many verbs are actor-flexible; value = *typical* addressee.
5. **bindingness** — `binding` (decides/authorizes when SC or on internal/budget matters), `conditional` (requests/decides addressed to Secretariat = operationally executable but domestic-law-neutral), `hortatory` (calls upon/urges/demands to States in GA), `none` (preambular/expressive). Per Namibia (B1): this is *presumptive*, context can override.
6. **budget_relevant** — boolean flag: does this verb+construction typically trigger a PBI / create a resource or reporting mandate? True mainly for deciding + directive-to-Secretariat verbs.

## Part G — Where sources disagree

1. **"also" vs "further" ordering.** DGACM official guide (A1): *also* for the 2nd repeat, *further* for the 3rd+. Many MUN guides and even the WebSearch summary of the DGACM manual say the reverse (*further* 2nd, *also* 3rd+). The DGACM delegate PDF is authoritative; trust "also-then-further."
2. **Is "decides" one notch up or a different kind?** UN.org/MUN guides put it at the top of one strength ladder; legal sources (A5, B4) treat it as a *category jump* into bindingness. The taxonomy resolves this by separating `force_rank` from `bindingness`.
3. **Grey-area verbs.** "calls upon," "requests," "warns" — legal literature (B4) explicitly says their force is *not* settled; MUN guides assign them fixed ranks. Mark these as low-confidence on the bindingness dimension.
4. **"demands" = SC only?** Widely stated as convention but not a written rule; GA has occasionally used stronger language. **[UNCERTAIN]**
5. **List membership varies.** DGACM's own two lists (A1 vs A2 examples) and the Model-UN list (A3) don't fully coincide (A3 adds Directs, Proclaims, Repeals, Suggests, Considers as operative; omits Condemns/Demands/Deplores). No single closed canonical list exists post-Bulletin-313.

## Part H — Critique of the draft 5-category list (observing / reinforcing / evaluative / deciding / directive)

**What it gets RIGHT:**
- The five buckets map well onto Searle's illocutionary classes + a repetition operator: observing≈assertive, evaluative≈expressive, deciding≈commissive/declarative, directive≈directive. This is a theoretically defensible spine, and it matches how the DGACM annex implicitly groups verbs.
- Correctly separates *evaluative* (welcomes/condemns) from *directive* — these are genuinely different speech acts, and only directive/deciding create mandates. Good instinct.
- Correctly isolates *deciding* as its own class — that's exactly the mandate/bindingness-bearing set (matches A/60/733, Rule 153, Namibia).
- "reinforcing" usefully captures reaffirm/recall/reiterate, which DGACM treats specially (anaphoric, must point to prior text). Recognizing them as a distinct behavior is astute.

**What it gets WRONG / needs fixing:**
1. **Missing dimensions.** A single 5-way category can't express force ordering, bindingness, addressee, or budget-relevance — the four things that actually matter for a mandate taxonomy. The draft needs the orthogonal dimensions in Part F1. Force and category are different axes (e.g. invites, urges, demands are all "directive" but span the whole force range).
2. **"reinforcing" is not a peer category** — it's an operator layered on other categories. "reaffirms [its condemnation]" is reinforcing+evaluative; "recalls [resolution X]" is reinforcing+observing. underline/underscore/emphasize/stress/highlight are arguably *evaluative-emphatic*, not the same as reaffirm/recall (which are cross-resolution links). Consider splitting: (a) anaphoric-repetition (reaffirm/reconfirm/recall/reiterate) vs (b) emphasis (underline/emphasize/stress/highlight).
3. **Misfiled verbs:**
   - **"support"** is placed in evaluative; when operative ("Supports the efforts of …") it borders on endorsement/deciding. Ambiguous — flag.
   - **"declare"** and **"resolve"** in *deciding* are more *declarative/commissive* than budget-generating; they rarely create executable mandates. Fine to keep but low budget-relevance.
   - **"endorse"** and **"approve"** belong in deciding (correct) but are the *budget-critical* ones — the draft doesn't flag that.
   - **"recommend"** is in *directive* (correct as speech act) but it's the classic **inter-organ, non-binding** verb — its addressee is usually another organ, unlike request/urge/demand aimed at States/SG.
   - **"remind"** (directive) is really closer to *reinforcing* in function (reminds States of existing obligations) — hybrid.
4. **"confirm" vs "reaffirm/reconfirm"** — the draft puts "confirm" in observing and "reconfirm" in reinforcing; these are near-synonyms differing only in anaphora. Keep consistent.
5. **Polarity is invisible.** Evaluative lumps welcomes (+), condemns (−), notes-with-concern (−). A sentiment axis is needed to make the category useful.
6. **"decides to remain seized" / "expresses its intention"** — the draft's deciding bucket would over-weight these as strong; they are near-empty procedural/forward-signalling clauses (force ~1–2, no mandate). Worth a low-force sub-tag.
7. **Coverage gaps** vs the official DGACM list: the draft omits **authorizes** (critical — it's the prime SC/budget mandate verb), **approves**, **adopts** is present but **proclaims/directs/appeals/suggests** are missing, and **demands** appears only in "directive" without noting it's the top-force binding-in-SC verb.

**What surprised us:**
- There is **no single official closed list** of resolution verbs; everyone points back to **1978 Terminology Bulletin 313**, which is not readily available online. The living authority is the DGACM Editing Section's delegate guidance (A1).
- The DGACM guide treats several "verbs" as **editorial-rule-bearing**, not free choices: reaffirm/recall/reiterate *must* point to prior text; "takes note" vs "notes" depends on the object; a body may not "welcome/take note of" its own past decision. So some category assignments are *enforced by UN editors*, which is a strong external validation signal for a taxonomy.
- **Bindingness is deliberately not verb-deterministic** (Namibia): the strongest legal authority says read the whole context. A verb-only classifier will therefore be *presumptive*, and that's the correct, defensible framing.
- The **addressee** turns out to be as important as the verb for both bindingness and budget impact — "requests" is hortatory to a State but an executable, PBI-triggering mandate when addressed to the Secretary-General.
